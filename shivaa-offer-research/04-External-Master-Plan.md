## 2. The Central Legal Risk — Lottery vs. Legal Prize Contest

### 2.1 Why the scheme as described is legally a "lottery"

Indian law tests three ingredients to decide if something is an illegal lottery: (1) a **prize**, (2) distributed by **chance**/lot, and (3) **consideration** — the participant has to pay or buy something to be in the draw. Your plan has all three: the 100g gold biscuit is the prize, the winner is picked in a live draw (chance), and entry requires a minimum ₹5g gold / 100g silver purchase (consideration). That combination is what Section 2(b) of the Lotteries (Regulation) Act, 1998 and Section 294-A of the Indian Penal Code target, and it is separately called out as an "unfair trade practice" under Section 2(47) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — conducting a lottery or a contest of skill or chance to promote the sale of goods.

Several states go further and specifically ban any prize draw tied to a purchase, however it is labelled (Tamil Nadu's Prize Scheme Prohibition Act is the strictest example; Maharashtra taxes/regulates such schemes separately under its own Lotteries and Prize Competitions law). Because shivaa.in sells and ships pan-India, you are potentially exposed to the strictest state law that applies to any customer who enters — not just Rajasthan's law.

*⚠ This is a genuine criminal-law exposure (imprisonment is a stated penalty under Section 294-A IPC and under the Lotteries Act), not just a fine-and-move-on risk. With a longer, higher-visibility campaign now running for 3.5 months, the exposure window is also longer — one more reason to fix the mechanic before, not during, the campaign.*

### 2.2 The quiz is the right instinct — make it genuinely skill-based

- The quiz needs objectively right/wrong answers or a scored/judged element (e.g. a numeric tie-breaker like "guess the closing MCX gold rate on 31 Dec", or a creative caption/photo contest judged by a panel) — not trivia everyone can answer correctly.
- Winners should be determined by quiz performance (best score / closest guess / best judged answer), not by a random draw among everyone who merely attempted the quiz.
- Publish the judging criteria and process in writing, and follow them exactly — inconsistent judging is itself treated as an unfair trade practice.

### 2.3 The essential structural fix: a genuine "no purchase necessary" route

- Every legally-run Indian promotional contest (bank offers, FMCG "scan and win", e-commerce app contests) keeps one alternate entry route that does not require any purchase, with **identical odds** of winning. This removes "consideration" from the legal equation and is what allows the campaign to run as a contest rather than a lottery.
- Add a clearly visible "Enter free, no purchase necessary" link/form on shivaa.in and in every Instagram post, using the same quiz and the same odds as paying entrants.
- This is close to the standard legal test applied to these campaigns — treat it as non-negotiable, not a nice-to-have.

### 2.4 Advertising compliance — the "100% FREE" wording

- The CCPA's Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements, 2022 (under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019) prohibit describing something as "free" if the consumer has to pay anything beyond an unavoidable cost to get it. Since a minimum purchase is required, calling the gold biscuit "100% free" is a misleading claim as worded, and can draw a CCPA penalty of up to ₹10 lakh (up to ₹50 lakh on repeat violation), in addition to consumer complaints.
- Reword every script/creative: "Win a 100g gold biscuit" / "Stand a chance to win" / "One lucky winner gets..." — with the purchase condition and full terms stated in the same breath, not buried in fine print.
- State the prize value as an estimate tied to the gold rate ("value at today's rate approx. ₹15 lakh, will vary with the gold price on the draw date") — with a 3.5-month gap between announcement and the 31 Dec draw, the actual value of 100g gold could differ meaningfully from ₹15 lakh by year-end; do not lock in a guaranteed rupee figure.
- All material conditions — minimum purchase, one entry per person/KYC, odds, exact draw date and process, how the winner is verified and contacted, and who bears the tax — must be stated clearly enough for a viewer to judge their real chances, not just in a linked T&C page.

### 2.5 Mid-campaign compliance discipline

A 3.5-month, high-visibility campaign draws more sustained attention than a 30-day one. Build in a compliance checkpoint roughly every 4–5 weeks: review live ad creatives against the CCPA wording rules, check the grievance log, and confirm the free-entry route is still functioning and equally promoted — not quietly deprioritised once purchases start flowing in.

## 3. Tax Compliance on the Prize

### 3.1 The winner's tax liability — 30% flat, deducted at source

Winnings from a lottery, prize draw, contest, or game of any kind are taxed under Section 115BB of the Income Tax Act at a flat 30% (plus applicable cess, effectively ~31.2%), regardless of the winner's income slab, with no deductions allowed against it. The company distributing the prize is legally required to deduct this tax before releasing the prize, under the TDS provision formerly numbered Section 194B (renumbered Section 393(3) Sl.1 under the new Income Tax Act effective 1 April 2026 — same rate and rule, new section number only).

Because the entire prize is in kind (gold, not cash), the winner cannot pay TDS out of the prize itself. The law requires either: the winner deposits the TDS amount in cash before collecting the gold, or the company "grosses up" and bears the tax itself. State this decision clearly in the T&C before launch — do not leave it to be negotiated live on camera at the hand-over.

| Scenario | How it works | Approximate numbers (at ₹15L prize value) |
|---|---|---|
| A — Winner bears the tax (recommended, simpler) | Winner deposits 30% TDS in cash before collecting the biscuit; company remits it as TDS on the winner's PAN | Winner deposits ~₹4,50,000 cash; company's cost stays at the ₹15,00,000 gold value |
| B — Company bears/grosses up the tax | Company pays the TDS on the winner's behalf; that payment is itself treated as an additional benefit and gets taxed again (grossing-up formula: value ÷ (1 − 30%)) | Grossed-up value ≈ ₹21,42,857; additional tax the company effectively absorbs ≈ ₹6,42,857 on top of the ₹15,00,000 gold |

Collect the winner's PAN before the draw is finalised — a materially higher TDS rate applies if PAN is not furnished, and the winner may need to be barred from collecting the prize until KYC/PAN is complete.

File TDS returns and issue the correct Form 26AS/16A entries — treat this like any other statutory TDS compliance with the usual quarterly return and challan deadlines.

### 3.2 Section 194R does NOT apply here

Section 194R (10% TDS on business perquisites) applies only when the recipient is themselves carrying on a business or profession and the benefit arises from that business relationship (e.g. gifts to dealers, distributors, or doctors). A retail customer winning a consumer lucky draw is not covered by Section 194R — the correct provision is 194B/115BB above. Keep this distinction clear for your CA so the wrong section isn't applied in your books.

## 4. GST & Cost Structuring

Gold given away without separate consideration is generally treated as a "gift" for GST purposes, which triggers input tax credit (ITC) reversal on the corresponding purchase under Section 17(5)(h) of the CGST Act — ITC cannot be claimed (or must be reversed if already claimed) on gold bought specifically to give away free. Whether the biscuit is booked as a standalone marketing/business-promotion expense (with ITC reversed) or structured into a composite/mixed supply bundled with qualifying purchases changes your GST outflow meaningfully.

Get a written CA opinion modelling both structuring options in rupee terms before committing to purchase the biscuit — with a 3.5-month gap to the draw, there is time to do this properly rather than reactively.

The ₹9,00,000 marketing spend is a normal deductible business-promotion expense; the gold biscuit's GST treatment is the more delicate, higher-value item to get right.

## 5. E-commerce, Product & Website Compliance

### 5.1 Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020

- Display your legal entity name, CIN, registered address, GSTIN, and a named Grievance Officer with contact details and a stated response-time commitment — mandatory for any e-commerce entity.
- Clear, prominent return/refund/exchange/cancellation policy, delivery timelines, and payment/security details.
- Country of origin and full price break-up (making charges, GST) shown before checkout.

### 5.2 BIS Hallmarking

- Gold jewellery sold on shivaa.in must carry HUID hallmarking. With 200 external manufacturers' designs on the platform, confirm each manufacturer's hallmarking compliance before listing — liability for a non-hallmarked or under-carat item sold through your site sits with you as the seller of record.
- Silver hallmarking is not yet fully mandatory nationwide, but disclose purity clearly (e.g. BIS 925) — the promotional "100g silver" minimum-purchase line should be purity-declared, not generic silver.

### 5.3 Data protection — DPDP Act, 2023

You will collect personal data at scale: quiz answers, purchase history, PAN for TDS, delivery details, and video/photo of the live draw. Publish a clear, consent-based privacy policy compliant with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, including retention period and a deletion-request process.

## 6. IP & Manufacturer Licensing (200 Bombay Manufacturers)

- Get a written agreement with each manufacturer (or one standard-form agreement all 200 sign) covering: ownership of product images/designs, whether Shivaa buys outright, takes on consignment, or acts purely as a listing/marketplace, liability to the end customer for defects, and the GST invoicing flow (manufacturer → Shivaa → customer, or manufacturer → customer with Shivaa as facilitator) — these have materially different GST and Companies Act consequences.
- Confirm rights to reproduce manufacturers' photographs/designs at scale (4 lakh+ SKUs) — using another business's product photos or original designs without permission can trigger a copyright/Designs Act dispute, separate from whether the jewellery itself is genuine.
- With the campaign now running to 31 December, there is time to onboard manufacturers in structured batches rather than all at once — sequence this so the highest-demand categories (bridal, daily-wear gold, festive silver) are live and hallmark-verified before the public campaign launch, with the long tail of SKUs following through the campaign window.

## 7. Consumer Court & Dispute Risk

- Publish complete, unambiguous Terms & Conditions before launch: eligibility, one-entry-per-person/KYC rule, exact draw date/time/method, how the winner is verified and contacted, what happens if the winner is unreachable, tax liability (Section 3), and the free-entry alternate route.
- Have a practising Chartered Accountant or Notary Public witness and certify the live draw process, and keep the full unedited recording — standard practice so a disappointed participant's complaint can be defended with documentary proof.
- Under the CPA 2019, false/exaggerated prize-value claims, unclear odds, or a rigged-seeming process can be treated as unfair trade practice by the CCPA (fines up to ₹50 lakh on repeat violation), independent of any individual consumer's court case.
- A 3.5-month campaign will generate a steadier stream of customer-service load than a 30-day one — keep the grievance/refund process visibly staffed and responsive for the full duration, not just around launch.

## 9. Complete Positives & Negatives

### 9.1 Legal & Compliance

**Positives**
- A well-documented, lawyer-reviewed mechanic (skill contest + free entry) converts a legal liability into a defensible, industry-standard promotion.
- The extra runway to 31 December gives real time to get every compliance document — T&C, privacy policy, grievance process, manufacturer agreements — right rather than rushed.

**Negatives**

- A longer, higher-visibility campaign has a longer window in which an ad-wording slip or a mishandled complaint can be flagged.
- Pan-India shipping exposes the scheme to the strictest state-level prize-scheme law that applies to any entrant, not just Rajasthan's.

### 9.2 Financial

**Positives**
- A single flagship prize is a capital-efficient way to generate reach compared to sustained discounting across the whole catalog.
- The larger ₹9,00,000 budget over 3 months allows proper phased spending (teaser → peak → retargeting) instead of front-loading everything into a rushed window.
- New customer acquisition and catalog-browsing traffic from the campaign has value to shivaa.in beyond the single winner.

**Negatives**
- Total campaign cost (Section 16) is materially higher than the headline ₹15 lakh + ₹9 lakh once TDS/tax, legal, CA, insurance, and manufacturer-onboarding costs are included.
- Gold price movement between now and 31 December is a real variable — the biscuit could cost more than ₹15 lakh to buy by year-end, and this should be budgeted as a range, not a fixed number.
- If Scenario B (company bears the tax) is chosen, the effective giveaway cost rises by roughly ₹6.4 lakh.

### 9.3 Brand & Marketing

**Positives**
- Timed through the festive season into the new year, the campaign spans multiple gifting/buying occasions, not just one festival, widening the pool of relevant buyers.
- The 200-manufacturer, 4-lakh-design catalog combined with the Gemini recommendation chatbot gives customers a genuine reason to return to shivaa.in beyond the giveaway.
- A transparent, well-run live draw (CA/notary witnessed) can itself become a trust-building content moment for a brand-new website.

**Negatives**
- A live-streamed draw is unforgiving — any perception of unfairness, technical hiccup, or unreachable "winner" plays out publicly, and in a trust-sensitive category like gold this can do more brand damage than the campaign's upside.
- Only one winner nets the prize from potentially thousands of qualifying purchasers; if the odds aren't communicated clearly, this is exactly the kind of "false sense of opportunity" the CCPA guidelines are designed to catch.

### 9.4 Operational / Execution

**Positives**
- 15.5 weeks is enough time to sequence legal fixes, then website/catalog build, then a phased marketing launch — rather than doing all three simultaneously under pressure.
- Manufacturer onboarding can happen in structured batches instead of a single risky bulk integration.

**Negatives**
- Integrating 200 manufacturers' catalogs (4 lakh+ SKUs), a working chatbot, and full e-commerce compliance is still a large build even with more time — scope creep or delay in any one stream can compress the marketing window that follows it.
- A longer campaign means a longer period of elevated customer-support and fulfilment load on a website and team that are simultaneously still being built out.
