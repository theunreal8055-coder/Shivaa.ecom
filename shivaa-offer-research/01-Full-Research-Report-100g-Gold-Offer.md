## 4. Headline legal verdict (one page for the financier)

> **A "buy a minimum of ₹1 lakh, then win a free gold biscuit purely by chance" scheme is legally the riskiest possible shape.** In India, a lottery = **Prize + Chance + Consideration**. Your scheme has all three. Private lotteries are illegal; gambling is state-regulated and mostly banned; the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 brands "contests/lotteries to promote sales" and "prizes that look free but are built into the price" as **unfair trade practices** punishable by fine and imprisonment; and a ₹15-lakh prize triggers TDS of ~30%+ before you can hand over the gold.

> **However**, the same commercial goal is achievable with low legal risk by restructuring just two things:
> 1. **Entry must not require purchase** — offer a genuinely free, no-purchase entry route (e.g., free form entry on the website), which removes "consideration" and converts a lottery into a lawful promotional **sweepstake**; and/or
> 2. **Add a real skill element** — a genuine jewellery-knowledge quiz with a pass mark before entry (India's courts treat games of skill differently from chance), and
> 3. **Run it with lawyer-drafted Terms & Conditions**, state exclusions (Tamil Nadu prohibits purchase-linked prize schemes; West Bengal requires licensing), Meta's mandatory contest disclaimer, clean draw mechanics witnessed by an independent auditor, and full tax handling published in advance.

> In short: **the marketing hook can stay; the legal chassis must change.** Everything below is the detail behind this verdict.

## 5. Legal & regulatory deep-dive

### 5.1 Lottery law — why "buy-to-enter + random draw" is dangerous

- Indian law defines a lottery as a scheme for distribution of prizes **by lot or chance** to persons who participate **by purchasing tickets** (Lotteries (Regulation) Act, 1998). Courts analyse a "lottery" through three elements: **prize, chance, consideration**. When all three are present, a private lottery is illegal.
- Running a private lottery is a criminal offence under **Section 294A IPC** (punishable, and the state can forfeit instruments of the lottery). Only **state governments** may run lotteries, under strict conditions.
- NITI Aayog-type clarifications and the digital-payments cashback rulings do **not** bless a private company's "buy goods → random draw for a huge prize" scheme; they address small cashback for digital transactions where the "prize" is a discount-like benefit, not a ₹15 lakh random giveaway.
- **Practical industry reading:** many "buy & win" brand contests operate in a grey zone, and prosecutions of sales-promotion draws are rare when the customer genuinely receives the goods they paid for (LawRato, Kanoon summaries, Section 15). "Rare prosecution" is **not** a licence — one motivated competitor, consumer, or the CCPA can change that. For a ₹15 lakh prize being announced on Instagram Live, assume scrutiny, not rarity.

### 5.2 Prize-competition and state laws

- **Prize Competitions Act, 1955** (central): restricts prize competitions (traditionally puzzle-based competitions) — a licence/conditions regime bites when monthly prize value exceeds **₹1,000** or entries exceed **2,000**; violation can mean imprisonment up to 3 months and/or fine. Whether a pure lucky draw counts as a "prize competition" is debated — a lawyer must opine on your exact design (quiz + draw).
- **Tamil Nadu Prize Schemes (Prohibition) Act, 1979**: expressly prohibits prize schemes where the draw is among persons **who purchase goods** from the sponsor. **Practical rule used across India: exclude Tamil Nadu residents from entry** (state "void where prohibited" in terms).
- **West Bengal Gambling and Prize Competitions Act, 1957**: regulates prize competitions and requires licensing above thresholds. **Exclude West Bengal (or obtain the licence).**
- Other states (e.g., Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra, and your home state **Rajasthan** with its public-gambling law) have gambling statutes aimed at games of chance/prize schemes. Rajasthan's law primarily targets gambling houses and games of chance; whether a sales-promotion lucky draw falls within it must be confirmed by your lawyer because the campaign originates from Rajasthan (Jayal, Nagaur).
- **Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act, 1978:** your scheme is a normal goods sale + promotion, not a money-circulation scheme — but avoid any language ("invest ₹1 lakh, get…") that sounds like a return-on-investment promise.

**Compliance posture:** publish full T&C; exclude residents of prohibited states; keep entry open nationally but "void where prohibited".

### 5.3 Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — the law that will actually be used against you

**Unfair trade practice (Section 2(47))** includes:
- offering **gifts, prizes or other items with the intention of not providing them** as offered;
- creating the **impression that something is given free when it is fully or partly covered by the amount charged** in the transaction as a whole;
- **conducting any contest, lottery, game of chance or skill** for the purpose of promoting, directly or indirectly, the sale/use/supply of any product — except as prescribed;
- misleading advertisements / false representation.

**Penalties (enforced by the Central Consumer Protection Authority — CCPA):**
- False/misleading advertisement: fine up to **₹10 lakh and/or imprisonment up to 2 years**; on repeat, fine up to **₹50 lakh and/or up to 5 years** (Section 89).
- Non-compliance with CCPA directions: imprisonment up to 6 months and/or fine up to ₹20 lakh (Section 88).
- CCPA can also order recall, refund, discontinuation of the practice.

**Consumer-court exposure (the "consumer court lawyer" part):**
- Any buyer can file before the **District/State/National Consumer Commission** (jurisdiction: District up to ₹50 lakh claims; State ₹50 lakh–₹2 crore; National above ₹2 crore). A ₹15 lakh prize dispute sits in the District Commission range.
- Grounds likely to be alleged: (i) the draw is a disguised lottery, (ii) the "free" prize is marketing built into inflated making charges/prices, (iii) winner selection is not transparent, (iv) delay or default in delivering the biscuit, (v) TDS surprise ("I won ₹15L but had to pay ₹4.7L tax first"), (vi) quality/purity issues on catalogue products (unhallmarked, wrong weight) since 4 lakh designs from 200 vendors multiply quality risk.
- Consumer commissions can order refunds, replacement, compensation for mental agony, and costs; CCPA can act **suo motu**.
- **Defence you can build today:** auditable records (draw log, auditor certificate, live-stream archive), lawyer-drafted T&C agreed at checkout, published tax terms, hallmarked/BIS stock with correct weights and purity (your repo already has a HUID workflow — use it), and a functioning grievance officer. **Never promise a prize you have not bought and set aside.**

### 5.4 CA / Tax compliances (give this page to your CA)

**A. Income tax on the prize (winner side + your duty)**
- Lottery/game-show winnings are taxed under **Section 115BB** at a flat **30% + 4% health & education cess** (≈31.2% effective) with **no exemption, no deduction, no basic-exemption set-off**.
- **Section 194B TDS:** you (the payer) must deduct TDS at ~30% (+cess as applicable) before paying/crediting a prize above **₹10,000**. For a **prize in kind** (gold), the accepted practice is: value the biscuit at fair market value on the draw date, compute tax (~31.2%), and **the winner pays the tax before taking delivery** (you deposit it as TDS). Collect the winner's **PAN**; without PAN, additional 20% TDS applies under **Section 206AA** — make PAN mandatory in the T&C.
- Practical handling used by brands: you deduct and deposit TDS within the statutory timeline, issue the TDS certificate (Form 16A / reflected in 26AS), and the winner declares it under "Income from Other Sources". If you decide to **absorb the tax** ("winner gets full biscuit, we pay tax"), that grossing-up itself has tax consequences — your CA must model both options. **Publishing "~30% statutory TDS payable by winner" before the draw is essential** to avoid the misleading-ad claim of "100% free".

**B. GST**
- GST rates (unchanged since 2017): **3%** on gold/silver value (1.5%+1.5% or 3% IGST); making charges on jewellery attract **5%** in practice per supplier billing (confirm with your CA — CBIC guidance says 3% on the retail transaction; some invoices split making charges at 5%).
- **You must be GST-registered** to sell and invoice jewellery online. Repo state shows CIN and UDYAM exist but **GSTIN is empty — obtain your GSTIN immediately** (Section 9.2). Every Mumbai supplier invoice must carry their GSTIN; you need supplier GSTINs for ITC and genuineness checks.
- **Free gift & ITC:** giving away the ₹15 lakh biscuit without consideration is generally not a taxable supply, but the **input tax credit on it may be restricted/needs reversal** (gifts above ₹50,000 per recipient per year exceed the rule's exception). Since gold businesses also face special ITC rules, your CA must decide the cleanest purchase/booking route (e.g., buy as stock-in-trade, treat the giveaway as documented business promotion). This can be several lakhs of rupees of credit — get it right.
- **TCS on large purchases (Section 206C(1H)/194Q):** once your purchases from suppliers cross thresholds in a FY, TCS/TDS on purchase provisions kick in — relevant at scale (₹3 crore GMV ambitions).
- Your own gold/silver purchases from bullion dealers: keep purchase invoices, assay/purity certificates, and BIS hallmark records — they are your proof of genuine goods and your defence in any purity complaint.

**C. Company & registrations**
- CIN `U32111RJ2025PTC099173` (Pvt Ltd, incorporated 2025) and UDYAM registration exist — good. Obtain: GSTIN (Rajasthan), shop/establishment (if applicable), trade licence of Nagaur/Jayal municipality, and confirm PAN/TAN (for TDS on the prize). If turnover crosses limits, also check professional-tax registration.
- Keep statutory registers, board resolution **approving this offer and the ₹15 lakh prize** (directors' authority), and minutes — a financier will ask.

### 5.5 BIS hallmarking & HUID (product compliance — gold & silver)

- **Gold hallmarking is mandatory** for jewellery/artefacts sold in notified districts (phased since 2021; HUID per piece mandatory since **1 April 2023**). Allowed gold purity marks: 14K/18K/22K (916). The CMS already has a HUID workflow — every listed gold design needs a real BIS logo + purity + **HUID** verifiable via the BIS Care app. **Automatic BIS verification is not connected** (per repo state) — that's a process gap to close for a 4-lakh-SKU ambition.
- **Silver hallmarking is voluntary** under IS 2112:2025 (since 1 Sep 2025) and under government review for mandatory status — but for a trust-led campaign, treat silver hallmarking as your standard anyway (925/999 marks).
- **The prize biscuit:** a 24K **biscuit/coin** is sold with a **refiner's certificate of purity** (e.g., MMTC-PAMP, India Government Mint) rather than a jewellery hallmark; check whether the mandatory-hallmark order covers the format you gift, and buy only from a certified refiner that provides the certificate + invoice. This certificate is your proof if anyone questions "is it really 999 pure?".
- Selling jewellery to customers across India means delivering into notified districts — your suppliers (200 Mumbai manufacturers) must warrant in writing that every piece is hallmarked with HUID and pure as described. **Your repo rule "never fabricate weight/purity/price" is exactly right — extend it contractually to suppliers.**

### 5.6 E-commerce consumer rules (Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020)

As an **inventory e-commerce entity** selling on your own website, you must, among other things:
- Display: **legal entity name**, registered office address, **GSTIN**, customer-care and grievance-officer contact details, and website details — "in a clear and accessible manner" on the platform.
- **Appoint a Grievance Officer** (name, email, phone displayed) + a nodal contact person resident in India.
- **Acknowledge complaints within 48 hours; redress within 30 days.**
- Display return/refund/exchange/warranty/delivery/payment/fees/charges information before purchase.
- Not make misleading representations; not falsely represent reviews; honour advertised warranty/guarantee.
- **Key for your offer:** every campaign page must state the full scheme mechanics, entry routes, eligibility, prize, taxes, draw method and dates — this doubles as both Meta compliance and Consumer-Rules transparency.
- (2025 amendments/regulatory proposals to e-commerce rules were under discussion; check current status with your lawyer before launch.)

### 5.7 Advertising & platform (Instagram/Meta) compliance

- **ASCI Code** (self-regulation, enforceable in spirit via CCPA): advertisements must be truthful; "free" claims must genuinely be free; the rupee-value claim for gold must be current; disclaimers must be legible, in the same language, and not contradict the main claim.
- **CCPA/DoCA guidelines on misleading ads** apply to social media too — including "bait and switch" and exaggerated prize claims.
- **Meta/Instagram promotion rules** require, for any contest/giveaway run from a Business/Creator account:
  - Official rules published (eligibility incl. age/geography, dates with time zone, prize, entry method, selection method, winner notification).
  - The **exact disclaimer**: "This promotion is in no way sponsored, endorsed or administered by, or associated with Instagram."
  - No inaccurate tagging; personal-account promotions are disallowed; do **not** make "share to your Story" or "tag 10 friends" the *only* entry mechanic (provide the official entry via your landing page/rules link).
  - Compliant influencer promos require #ad disclosures.
  - Save entry records and proof for at least 90 days (longer is safer: keep 3+ years with your legal records).
- **Live announcement risk:** announcing "100% free gold, worth ₹15 lakh, just buy ₹1 lakh" on a live stream is precisely the claim that gets clipped and re-shared against you. Script it with the compliant wording (Section 11).

### 5.8 Data protection & privacy (customers + winner)

- **DPDP Act, 2023** (implementation evolving through 2025–26): you need a privacy policy, consent notices for the quiz/chatbot/entry form, purpose limitation, and data-request handling. The prize draw collects phone/PAN/address — treat as personal data; publish how it will be used (and how winner details will be published — get explicit consent or publish only first name + city as is customary).
- OTP verification at entry (India mobile numbers) doubles as fraud control.
- Chatbot (Gemini) usage: display that an AI assistant is in use; do not let it promise the draw outcome, "guaranteed returns", or product facts it cannot verify.

### 5.9 Payments, fraud & financial
- Payment gateway KYC and settlement agreements (order value ~₹1 lakh — normal e-commerce risk; beware chargebacks and card fraud inflating "qualifying orders").
- Anti-fraud: one entry per verified phone/PAN, reject bulk/bot orders (same address/PAN patterns), refund-without-entry for cancelled orders, cut-off rules (orders must be *delivered* or just *paid*? — define in T&C; "paid and not cancelled/refunded within the cooling window" is cleaner than "delivered").
- The company must keep the prize biscuit **separately in stock (earmarked) before the draw** — a board-level decision and a compliance proof.

### 5.10 Contracts with the 200 manufacturers / suppliers
For every supplier onboarding (this is business-critical and legally protective):
1. Written **supply agreement**: design listing licence (who owns the design IP; warranty the design is original and does not infringe third-party IP/copyright/design registration), photography/description rights for your site, price + weight + purity guarantee, **hallmark/HUID warranty**, delivery timelines, returns/defect policy, liability for wrong/hallmarked-less supply.
2. **Supplier GSTIN + PAN on file**, KYC, bank account matching; written confirmation they are manufacturers/wholesalers registered appropriately.
3. Quality gate: sample-based QC before going live; **do not list what you cannot actually deliver** — the fastest way to kill the whole campaign with consumer complaints is to sell 4 lakh listings you can't fulfil.
4. Design IP caution: Mumbai market designs are frequently copied; selling a copy of a registered design can bring Design Act/Copyright claims against *you* as the seller — the supplier agreement must indemnify you.

### 5.11 Structure options — pick one before drafting T&C

| Option | Mechanics | Legal risk | Marketing effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Buy-to-enter lucky draw** (your current shape) | Purchase ₹1L+ → quiz → random draw | **High** — looks like lottery (prize+chance+consideration); TN/WB issues; CCPA "contest to promote sale" exposure | Strongest short-term purchase incentive |
| **B. Hybrid: purchase perk + free-entry sweepstake** (recommended) | Draw open to ALL via free website entry; verified purchasers get extra perks (instant cashback/discount/loyalty points or bonus entries) but a free non-purchase route always exists | **Low-Medium** — no pure lottery; still needs T&C, state exclusions, transparency | Slightly weaker "must buy" pull, but safer; buyers still heavily incentivised by discounts & bonus entries |
| **C. Open sweepstake + separate purchase offers** | Giveaway is a pure audience/lead magnet; purchases driven by Diwali offers, not by the draw | **Lowest** | Cleanest legally; draw grows followers; purchase engine is separate |

**Recommendation: Option B or C**, with the quiz as an optional "bonus entry + lead-qualification" step rather than the gate to a paid-entry draw. This preserves your Instagram story and chatbot quiz while removing the criminal-law core of the scheme.

## 6. Terms & Conditions — must-have clauses (draft with your lawyer)

1. Promoter's legal identity, address, GSTIN, contact, grievance officer.
2. Entry period (dates + IST), entry methods (paid route if any + **free route**), maximum entries per person.
3. **Eligibility**: 18+, India residents, exclusion of employees/relatives of Shivaa, suppliers and agencies; **"Void where prohibited — residents of Tamil Nadu and West Bengal excluded"** (unless licences obtained); if you sell/deliver only in certain states, say so.
4. The **prize**: 100 g 24K gold biscuit, purity certificate, current market value wording (no fixed rupee promise), tax treatment (~30%+ TDS; winner pays before delivery; PAN mandatory), delivery timeline (e.g., 30 days after verification), no cash alternative.
5. Draw method: audited random selection (independent auditor, recorded, live-streamed), date/time; winner verification (ID + PAN); what happens if winner unresponsive/forfeits (re-draw after X days).
6. Qualifying-order rules (thresholds in grams — gold min 5 g, silver min 100 g — per order; cancellations/refunds void entry; fraud disqualification).
7. Data use and privacy policy link.
8. Meta disclaimer line (Section 5.7) where relevant.
9. Force majeure, prize substitution if the gold format becomes unavailable, governing law & jurisdiction (Rajasthan), dispute resolution.
10. **No-purchase entry instructions** and how to enter free (email/WhatsApp/postcard) with equal chance.

## 7. Positives & negatives — of doing and of NOT doing (financier's table)

### 7.1 Positives of doing it
- **Festive timing**: a ₹15 lakh headline on Instagram during Dhanteras–Diwali can generate outsized attention vs. a ₹9 lakh ad budget alone; the prize IS the media.
- **Customer acquisition**: qualifying buyers are high-value (₹1L+). Even 200–300 such customers, at realistic festive CAC, would normally cost a jeweller far more than the campaign budget.
- **Data asset**: quiz answers + purchase history build a preference dataset for the AI/chatbot and retargeting.
- **First-mover credibility**: live, audited draw + BIS/HUID transparency builds the "modern, trustworthy jeweller" story your repo/trust page is already building (CIN/UDYAM shown; add GSTIN + certificate).
- **Demonstrates the platform**: 200 manufacturers + 4 lakh designs is your moat story; the campaign forces the site, catalogue and Ops to be launch-ready.
- **Financier signal**: executing a well-researched, compliant launch with clear numbers de-risks your pitch.

### 7.2 Negatives / drawbacks of doing it
- **Legal exposure** (Sections 5.1–5.3): lottery optics, CCPA fines, consumer complaints; needs lawyer + structure change before launch.
- **Cash outlay is heavy and front-loaded**: prize ₹15L (and if gold rises, more), TDS deposit ~₹4.7L (if absorbed/arranged), ads ₹9L, tech/legal/CA/ops ₹4–6L → **₹30L+ committed before results**.
- **Price risk**: gold/silver volatility changes prize cost and qualification thresholds mid-campaign.
- **Ops risk**: 4-lakh listings, 200 vendors, festive logistics, returns, and QC — a fulfilment failure poisons the draw's credibility.
- **Reputation risk**: "lucky draw = scam" skepticism; any hint of a rigged or delayed draw goes viral; the TDS surprise on the winner is a complaint magnet if not disclosed upfront.
- **Fraud risk**: fake/bot orders, cancellations after entry, chargebacks.
- **Compliance overhead**: T&C, grievance officer, GSTIN, hallmark guarantees, DPDP — all must exist before the public launch.

### 7.3 Positives of NOT doing it
- No legal/criminal-optics risk; no ₹30L exposure; time to perfect catalogue/QC/tech; can run a smaller, compliant launch (e.g., "Diwali cashback" or "gold coin with every ₹X purchase") and keep powder dry.
- Avoids the fixed-date trap of the current calendar confusion (31 Dec ≠ Bhai Dooj).

### 7.4 Negatives of NOT doing it
- Misses the single biggest jewellery demand window of the year (Dhanteras–Bhai Dooj) with no differentiating hook; competitors with similar stunts capture the festive audience; slower CAC growth; the financier sees research but no execution — exactly what he asked you to avoid.
